Shoppers ask about owning it before they buy it

A service blueprint with a third direction. Journey stages run across, who answers runs down, and depth shows how well each customer need is answered.

Example: Larkhob, a fictional premium kitchen brand with a made-to-order and limited-edition line, Larkhob Atelier. Its products, customers and questions are invented.

          How to read the map

          Across: time

          Pre-purchase, during and post-purchase, step by step, as in any service blueprint.

          Down: who answers

          The journey steps sit on top. Under them, each need sits in the lane that holds its answer: the brand, the brand with outside judgement, or outside the brand altogether.

          Depth: coverage

          A blueprint shows what the company does. Depth shows how close each need gets to a step that answers it. The further a need floats, the more the journey misses it, and its lane says who owns the fix.

          A new line: the line of authority

          Blueprints separate what customers see from what they don’t with a line of visibility. This map adds a line of authority, between answers the brand owns and answers that live elsewhere. When an AI assistant goes looking, that line decides whether the brand is the source or one voice among many. Placing needs in lanes is a researcher’s judgement; the model only measures closeness.

          How the map is made

          Two layers

          __NN__ customer needs written for Larkhob across its four categories, in the way people ask: with constraints, context and doubt. __NS__ blueprint steps across pre-, during- and post-purchase, worded as a shopper would say them.

          One space

          Each text is turned into a 768-number embedding with EmbeddingGemma, Google’s open embedding model, run locally through Ollama: no data leaves the machine and nothing is paid for. Closeness is cosine similarity. The 3D layout comes from t-SNE, which keeps neighbours together but bends long distances, so read clusters, not exact positions.

          What counts as unanswered

          Closeness alone measures resemblance, not whether a step answers a need, so each need is judged twice. EmbeddingGemma shortlists the five steps nearest to it, with each step described by the questions it is there to answer. Then Gemma 3, a small language model also run locally, reads the need and the shortlist and decides: a step answers it, a step partly answers it, or none does. It gives a one-line reason and, where nothing fits, names the step the journey is missing. Both are the model’s judgement, shown as such; a researcher can override any call.

          NN/g lenses

          Shopper types come from NN/g’s five types of ecommerce shoppers: product-focused, browsers, researchers, bargain hunters and one-time shoppers. They describe a shopping visit, so a sixth, Owner, is added for people caring for something they already have and value. Conversation types come from NN/g’s six types of conversations with generative AI: search query, funneling, exploring, chiseling, expanding and pinpointing. The coding of each need is a researcher’s judgement, not NN/g’s, and a second coder would test it.

          Price tier and delegation

          Each need is also coded by price tier, from mass through premium to luxury, and by what a shopper would let an agent do with it: act on it, help research it, or leave it alone. The delegation coding follows one argument: people hand over purchases that are replaceable and keep the ones that say something about them. Both are a researcher’s coding.

          Accessibility layers

          Access needs are coded in three layers: digital (the website, checkout and documents online), product (the item itself, its controls and instructions), and service and place (the shop, delivery, customer service and information in other formats). Everything else is coded as not about access, for comparison. This is a researcher’s coding.

          Levels, owners and actions

          Every unanswered need gets a level, an owner and a first action, by fixed rules shown with the action list. Access needs and needs an agent would act on are critical; answers that live outside the brand are high; the rest are medium. Owners follow the lane: digital access to the web team, product access to product design, service access to customer experience, outside answers to content and partnerships. The levels come from coding, not from volume, and a company should weight them with its own data.

          Where the answer lives

          Each need is also coded by where a good answer comes from: the brand’s own pages, outside sources such as reviews, specialists and regulators, or both. An AI assistant goes wherever the best answer is, so needs answered outside the brand are where the brand stops being the authority on its own product. This is a researcher’s coding too.

          About Larkhob

          Larkhob is a fictional kitchen brand, made up to show the method. Its Atelier line of numbered limited editions and made-to-order pieces gives the example its luxury needs; the journey steps were left as a premium shop would draw them. Its four categories, the journey steps and every customer need on this page are written for the example, not collected from real customers or taken from any company. Any resemblance to an existing brand, product or business is unintended. If you believe the name or content matches an existing brand, please get in touch and it will be changed.

          The judge’s prompt, by CARE

          Written with NN/g’s CARE structure for AI prompts.

          Context
          A strict reviewer of a kitchen brand’s customer journey, given one customer need and the five nearest steps.
          Ask
          Say what the customer needs to know, what the best step would give them, and whether it answers, partly answers or does not answer the need.
          Rules
          A shared topic or word is not enough. If a typical version of the step would leave the question open, it does not answer it. When unsure, say none. Look hard at needs about access, the body, rules and safety.
          Examples
          Three worked cases from other shops, a rug, a bike and a sofa, showing answers, partly and none.

          Twins against the researcher

          Thirty of the needs were put to digital twins of real US shoppers on ExploraTwin, the Columbia Business School Digital Twins Lab’s platform, using the Open e-commerce 1.0 panel. Each twin said where it would look first for an answer, and what it would let an AI assistant do. The twins test the researcher’s coding; they are not customers.

          Digital twins agree with real people only weakly at the level of the individual (the lab reports an average correlation of 0.20), and their answers vary less than people’s. Read the agreement as a check on the coding, not as evidence about shoppers. The panel is American; the wording was adapted for it.
